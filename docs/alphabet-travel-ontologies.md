# Making the alphabet travel — what's available, and what isn't

*Literature review · 29 August 2026 · polyx / expertise*
*Addresses the open problem in `expertise-concept.md` §10: a rule is a predicate over a vocabulary, and my `action:issue_refund` is your `refund.create`.*

---

## 1. The problem is three problems

Most discussion of "shared ontologies for agents" conflates layers that fail independently. Separating them is most of the work, because two are solved and one is not.

| Layer | Question | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | What *shape* is an event? What can it relate to? | **Solved.** Adopt, don't invent. |
| **Referential** | Which real-world thing does this term denote? | **Partly solved**, per domain, unevenly. |
| **Pragmatic** | Is this action irreversible? What counts as its guard? | **Nothing exists.** This is polyx's gap and polyx's contribution. |

The third row is the finding of this review. No ontology surveyed below carries consequence, reversibility, or "counts-as-verification-here" — and those are precisely the predicates a mined rule needs. Everything else can be borrowed.

---

## 2. Family A — upper / foundational ontologies

The maximalist bet: fix meaning at the top and let everything below inherit it.

- **BFO** — Basic Formal Ontology, standardised as **ISO/IEC 21838-2:2021**, currently BFO-2020. Continuant/occurrent at the root; formally careful; genuinely good work.
- **Common Core Ontologies (CCO)** — a mid-level layer over BFO covering agents, artifacts, events, information; the practical bridge most BFO deployments actually use.
- **DOLCE**, **SUMO**, **gist** — the other long-standing contenders, gist being the deliberately minimal business-oriented one.

**What they'd give polyx:** a principled place to say that an action is an occurrent with participants, and a shared root that makes two domain vocabularies formally comparable.

**Why I would not bet on them.** The adoption record is the argument. BFO is twenty-five years old, ISO-standardised, and its penetration outside biomedicine (OBO Foundry) and defence/intelligence is close to nil. Where it *did* win, it won because of a **forcing function** — journals and funders requiring OBO compliance for publication — not because the formalism persuaded anyone. Expertise sharing has no journal, no funder, and no regulator. A format that requires developers to learn continuant-versus-occurrent before publishing a rule set will not be published.

There is also a mismatch of purpose. Upper ontologies exist to make assertions *consistent*. polyx needs terms to be *co-referential enough to score a predicate*. Those are different bars, and the second is much lower — which is good news.

## 3. Family B — event and process standards

This is the closest structural fit, and the recommendation in §7 comes from here.

### OCEL 2.0 — object-centric event logs

Berti, Koren, Adams, Park, Knopp et al. (RWTH Aachen), spec released Oct 2023; OCEL 2.1 adds CSV and Parquet serialisations alongside XML, JSON and SQLite.

The metamodel: **events** (atomic, timestamped, typed, attributed) and **objects** (typed, attributed, and — new in 2.0 — with **attribute values that change over time**). Two relation kinds: **E2O**, event-to-object, *qualified* by the object's role in that event (the spec's example distinguishes "organizer" from "regular participant"); and **O2O**, object-to-object, also qualified ("part-of", "reports-to"), which OCEL 1.0 lacked entirely.

**Why this matters more than it looks.** Classic XES and OCEL 1.0 assume one case identifier per event. A CRM interaction has no such thing: a single "issue refund" event involves a customer, an order, a product, an agent, and a policy simultaneously. Forcing that into a single case id is exactly the modelling error that makes conversational event logs awkward, and it is the error OCEL 2.0 was built to fix.

More specifically for polyx: a recommendation rule's antecedent is a conjunction over *objects related to the current event*. E2O qualifiers are the binding surface that antecedent needs. This is not a loose analogy — it is the same shape.

### PROV-O — the W3C provenance ontology

`prov:Entity`, `prov:Activity`, `prov:Agent`, with `wasGeneratedBy`, `wasDerivedFrom`, `wasAttributedTo`, `used`.

Not a vocabulary for *what happened in a business process*, but the right vocabulary for **the evidence claim attached to a rule**. A mined rule is an Entity, generated by a mining Activity, derived from a corpus Entity, attributed to a polyx Agent. That is exactly the attestation the expertise bundle needs, and PROV-O gives it a standard, already-tooled shape for free rather than inventing a bespoke JSON blob.

Worth adopting for the `evidence/` half of the bundle even if nothing else here is used.

### XES

The older IEEE process-mining standard. Single-case, superseded for polyx's purposes by OCEL 2.0, but it is what BPIC 2017 and most public process-mining corpora are published in, so the ingestion adapter meets it regardless.

## 4. Family C — domain vocabularies, where the actual terms live

Structure without terms is empty. These are the places the terms exist.

### Banking

- **FIBO** — the Financial Industry Business Ontology, maintained by the **EDM Council**, published as OWL at `spec.edmcouncil.org/fibo` and developed openly on GitHub. Real, large, and genuinely used by large banks for data-model derivation (the FIB-DM line of work transforms it into enterprise data models).
- **ISO 20022** — and this is the more instructive one. A business model plus data dictionary for financial messaging, and it **won**, comprehensively, across payments infrastructure. It won because market infrastructures and regulators set migration deadlines. Forcing function again. But the practical point stands: for banking, the shared vocabulary already exists, is maintained by someone else, and your partners already speak it.

### Retail

- **schema.org**, including the **Actions** vocabulary (`potentialAction`, `TradeAction`, `TransferAction`, `OrderAction`) and **GoodRelations**, absorbed into schema.org for e-commerce.
- schema.org is the interesting case for the opposite reason to ISO 20022: **no forcing function, and it won anyway.** It was thin, it had a distribution channel (search engines), and it paid the publisher on day one with rich results. That combination — thin, channelled, selfishly worthwhile immediately — is the actual recipe, and it is the one available to an open expertise format.
- Notably, schema.org Actions is now being pushed as the "agent-ready website" surface, which means retail action terms are getting *more* canonical, not less.

---

## 5. Family D — agent-native, 2025–2026, and unsettled

- **Agent Data Protocol (ADP)** — 13 datasets, 1.3M trajectories unified under one released schema: three action types (api, code, message), two observation types (text, web). Deliberately thin. Already covered in the polyx literature review as the reader target, and it is the best-fitting *trace* schema for coding-agent work.
- **OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions** — consolidating agent spans and events into a standard shape, with vendor support. This is where operational agent telemetry is converging, and it has the one thing an ontology usually lacks: a channel (every observability vendor).
- **MCP / A2A** — protocol layers, not vocabularies. A2A's *agent card* describes capabilities for discovery; it says nothing about what an action *means*. Useful for finding an agent, useless for aligning two rule sets.
- **AgentO** (University of Vienna) and Skan AI's **Agentic Ontology of Work (AOW)** — early attempts at ontologies *of agentic systems themselves* (AOW: agents, skills, intents, contexts, policies, memory, confidence, outcomes; expressible as JSON-LD, RDF/OWL or property graphs). Interesting as evidence that others feel the same gap; too new and too unadopted to build on.

The honest summary of this family: everyone agrees a vocabulary is needed, nobody has one, and the two things with actual traction (ADP, OTel GenAI) are thin trace schemas rather than semantic vocabularies. That is a gap and also a warning about how hard the semantic part is.

## 6. The alignment mechanism, and its known failure

Even with shared structure, two vocabularies must be mapped. Two decades of **ontology matching** exist for this, benchmarked annually by the **OAEI**.

The lightweight instrument is **SKOS mapping properties** — `skos:exactMatch`, `closeMatch`, `broadMatch`, `narrowMatch`, `relatedMatch`. Deliberately weak logically, which is the point: they express "these two terms line up, roughly, in this direction" without committing to formal equivalence. For polyx this is almost certainly the right expressiveness. A rule does not need `owl:sameAs`; it needs "close enough that scoring this predicate is meaningful", which is `skos:closeMatch` almost exactly.

**LLM-based matching is now the active line** — OLaLa, GenOM, LLMs-as-oracles for alignment, prioritized depth-first search with LLMs — and it works well enough to be the obvious proposal mechanism.

**And it hallucinates, measurably.** `OAEI-LLM` exists specifically as a benchmark for LLM hallucination in ontology matching: the field built a dataset because the failure was common enough to need one. The implication is direct: **LLM-proposed mappings are candidates, never determinations.** Which is the same posture the polyx technical spec already takes toward rule-to-policy alignment, for the same reason.

## 7. What I'd actually do

Three layers, and the third is the only one polyx should invent.

**Layer 1 — transport shape: adopt OCEL 2.0.** Events, objects, typed, with qualified E2O and O2O relations and time-varying object attributes. It fits CRM interactions better than any single-case log, it has serialisations you don't have to design, and it comes from the community whose corpora you're already ingesting. Use PROV-O for the evidence/attestation half of the bundle. Invent neither.

**Layer 2 — term binding: optional, per-domain, SKOS-shaped.** An expertise declares its terms and *may* bind them to published IRIs — FIBO or ISO 20022 for banking, schema.org Actions for retail — via `exactMatch` / `closeMatch`. Unbound terms are legal and simply local. Optionality is not a compromise; it is what keeps the format publishable by someone who has never heard of FIBO, and the binding is what makes it travel when they have.

**Layer 3 — the pragmatic annotations, which nobody has.** For each term, the things a rule needs and no surveyed ontology carries:

```yaml
terms:
  - id: action:issue_refund
    binds: { closeMatch: "schema:ReturnAction" }
    consequential: true
    reversibility: costly        # irreversible | costly | free
    verifies: false
  - id: action:order_lookup
    binds: { closeMatch: "schema:SearchAction" }
    consequential: false
    verifies: true               # counts as a verification HERE
```

FIBO can tell you a loan offer is a loan offer. It cannot tell you that issuing one is irreversible, or that in *this* operation an order lookup is what counts as having checked. Those are the two facts every mined rule depends on, and they are exactly what is missing from the entire surveyed landscape. **That's the contribution, it's about six fields, and it's small enough to actually specify and get adopted.**

### And then the part that closes the loop

Install-time alignment is: LLM proposes mappings → human confirms → **and replay is the acceptance test.**

If you map their `refund.create` to your `action:issue_refund` and their rule then scores 41 of 44 against your corpus, the mapping is probably right. If it scores 0 of 31, either the rule doesn't hold here *or the mapping is wrong* — and the tool should say both, because it cannot distinguish them and pretending otherwise would be a lie.

**Replay validates the mapping, not just the rule.** The same mechanism that solved the trust problem in the expertise concept solves the alignment problem too, which is a good sign that the mechanism is load-bearing rather than convenient.

Finally: **mappings are themselves shareable artifacts.** A `schema.org ↔ acme-support` mapping written once benefits everyone installing that expertise. That amortises the cost the way DefinitelyTyped amortised TypeScript types, and it is a plausible community layer around a commercial miner.

---

## 8. What I would not do

- **Don't adopt an upper ontology.** Twenty-five years of evidence says the cost lands on authors and the benefit never arrives without a forcing function you don't have.
- **Don't build a complete CRM ontology.** FIBO took the EDM Council and a decade with the industry behind it. You are not going to do a better one as a side effect of a mining product.
- **Don't require binding.** The moment publishing an expertise requires resolving IRIs, publishing stops.
- **Don't trust an LLM mapping unconfirmed.** The field literally built a hallucination benchmark for this exact task.
- **Don't invent a trace format.** OCEL 2.0 and ADP exist; OTel GenAI is where telemetry is converging. Three is already too many, and a fourth from a startup is a footnote.

The lesson underneath all of it: schema.org and ISO 20022 both won, by opposite routes — one thin with a channel and an immediate selfish payoff, the other mandated with a deadline. You have no deadline to impose. So the only available route is thinness, a channel, and a day-one payoff — which is precisely the `expertise add` → `replay` moment, where a stranger's rules get scored against your own history in one command.
